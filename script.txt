Two hundred thousand dollars gone. Partner gone. I'm telling you what I did. I want you to think about this for a moment, because when it happened to me, I couldn't. I didn't have a moment. 

Four days later I had a phone call with a client; a team of six people were waiting for pay, and my bank account looked like someone had ripped it open with a shovel. There was no warning. There was no note. Derek wasn't there either.

But before I get to the point, I'll say something else. To understand what he took from me—and I'm not just talking about money—you have to understand where we're coming from.

Derek Hale and I grew up three houses apart on Manchaca Road in South Austin. We met when we were ten, in the summer of 2003, arguing about whose turn it was to play on the same basketball court behind Covington Middle School. 

He was stubborn from the start. I was even more so. We had exhausted each other so much that in the end there was nothing left but friendship.

Ten years. When my family broke up, it was camping with my family. When I found out about my dad, he called me at two in the morning. I was his best man. He was mine. It wasn’t a simple thing — it was the foundation on which everything else was built.

In January 2019, we started Web-Hell Logistics in a small co-working space on South Congress. The two of us had just fourteen thousand dollars and a whiteboard full of ideas that neither of us was sure would work. We coordinated last-mile deliveries for mid-sized e-commerce clients — that is, we bridged the gap between the big freight companies and the small merchants who couldn’t afford them. 

Derek did the accounting. I handled the relationships. It wasn’t complicated. It worked because we trusted each other completely, and in the early days, we had the experience of working together to back that trust.

We made our first million dollars in late 2021. We celebrated at a casual bar on East Sixth, the way we’d celebrated since we were teenagers—cheap beer, loud music, and no pretense. At one point, Derek grabbed my shoulder and said, “We really did it, man.” Not me. We. The word held special meaning for both of us.

The expansion phase was my plan, and Derek was a strong supporter. We were chasing regional deals — big customers, long-term commitments, and real infrastructure. To do that, we needed working capital that could move freely. 

That meant getting our finances together and getting our money flowing. Derek had been the bookkeeper from the very beginning, so it was natural that he would have full control over the accounts. It was natural because we had twenty years of history. It was natural because he was Derek. I know how that sounds now.

In early 2023, we were six weeks away from signing the biggest deal in the company’s history — a regional distribution deal with a mid-sized outdoor goods retailer in Dallas. Two hundred and fourty thousand dollars a year, renewable, the kind of deal that turns a struggling startup into something with a future. 

We had been working on that relationship for eight months. I was the one talking to them on the phone, going to their offices, and building that trust. Derek was behind the scenes making sure the financials were clear so we could close the deal. At least that’s what I believed.


The last time I saw Derek in person was on a Tuesday. He was sitting at his standing desk, headphones in, and the coffee next to his keyboard was getting cold as usual. As I was leaving for lunch with a client, he waved. A simple wave. As if nothing had happened.

Four days later, I checked my account. The number that flashed on my screen made no sense. I sat in my truck, phone in one hand and coffee in the other, and read the number twice. Three times. The operating account showed a balance of four thousand two hundred and twelve dollars.

We had over two hundred thousand dollars there four days earlier. Before I could close the banking app, I called Derek. It went straight to voicemail. I told myself that it was still morning, that he might be on the phone, and that a reasonable explanation was waiting on the other end of a text message. 

I sent a message: Hey, I need to talk about the account. Call me. Then I drove to the office, trying to keep my hands on the steering wheel.

He wasn’t there. His desk looked the same as before — the same monitor, the same dead plant in the corner that he kept saying he’d water — so for about thirty seconds I was able to convince myself that I might be exaggerating. Then I pulled up the transaction history.

Seven wire transfers in eleven days. The smallest was eighteen thousand dollars. The largest was sixty-one thousand four hundred dollars. All of them were from vendors I didn’t recognize—names that sounded out of place, like “Vantage Supply Solutions" and “TX Meridian Logistics LLC.” 

I’d never heard of any of them. We didn’t have a deal with any of them. I sat at my desk and scrolled through each one, a cold shiver running down my spine because I could already see what was coming. But I still couldn’t bring myself to say it. I called him again. Another voicemail.

That afternoon I drove to his apartment. I’d been there a hundred times—helped him move, watched the playoffs on his couch, and shared a bottle of whiskey in his backyard the night we signed our first real client. 

I knocked on the door. No answer. I knocked again. A neighbor I barely knew from the apartment next door came out and looked at me. “Are you his friend?” he said. “Yes.” “He left for the weekend. A truck came on Saturday.” I stood in the hallway for a moment. “Where did he say he was going?” He just shook his head.

I wanted to say I was calm. But I wasn’t. I sat in my truck in that parking lot and called him seven more times in a row, listening to the same voicemail greeting—his voice, simple and familiar, "Leave a message and I'll get back to you"—until I stopped calling because my hands were shaking so much I couldn't press the button properly.

That night I went through everything I could get my hands on. Bank records, vendor records, and email sources are attached to every transaction. "Vantage Supply Solutions" had a website that looked like it was two weeks old. 

"TX Meridian Logistics" had a registered address, which turned out to be a UPS store off Lamar Boulevard. Every one of those wire transfers had Derek's signature of approval. Every one.

Two hundred and sixty thousand dollars. Gone in eleven days. Sent in such small chunks that our automated alerts didn’t even register it as a single event. He knew exactly how to do it.

I got our lawyer on the phone around midnight, and after I told him what I was seeing, there was a long silence on the other end, then he said carefully, “Marcus. You need to file a police report first thing in the morning.”

I already knew that. What I didn’t know was how to call a client in Dallas and explain why our operating account had run out six weeks before we were supposed to close the biggest deal of our lives.

Payday was due on Friday. I had fourteen employees—drivers, dispatchers, and two operations coordinators—and the cash on hand was barely enough to cover maybe a third of it. I called my personal banker at seven in the morning, when he wasn’t at his desk as usual, and transferred every dollar of cash I had into the business account. 

My emergency fund. The money I had been saving since I was twenty-three because my father had told me never to touch it. Gone in a single wire transfer. It would have paid the salary. Somehow, I never told anyone why it was so low.

The Dallas client was even harder to deal with. Sandra Chu had been patient through two renegotiations and a six-week extension. She had trusted us because Derek had been impressing her for four months with steak dinners, site visits, and follow-up calls that she didn't even want. 

When I contacted him that Thursday morning, I told him that we had had an internal financial irregularity, which was now a legal matter. I used the word "irregularity" three times in forty seconds. He was silent for a moment, then said, "Marcus, is the company financially sound?"

I told him that we were taking this matter very seriously. He said that he would have to talk to his legal team. That was our last real conversation.

My attorney referred me to a forensic accountant named Renata Solis, who worked in a glass-and-concrete office on Congress Avenue and had a kind of steadiness about her that made it seem like she had seen everything twice. 

She asked for ninety days of records. I gave her everything we had—bank statements, vendor agreements, Vantage and TX Meridian incorporation documents, email chains, and wire confirmations. It was all printed out and packed in a banker’s box that I had spent most of a sleepless Wednesday organizing.

Two weeks later she sat down across from me at a small conference table and explained everything to me, drawing a timeline on a yellow legal pad. The first transfer had been four months ago. Not eleven days. 

Four months. At first, little by little—two thousand here, thirty-five there—were sent through a vendor account opened by Derek, whose name I didn’t recognize. Then, as the pattern continued, the larger sums began to flow. The last eleven days were just harvest days.

“He tested the system,” Renata said, tapping her legal pad. Each of those early transfers was a test. He was seeing if you would notice. I didn’t.

I was in Dallas courting Sandra Chu; in Houston, I was meeting with a freight company, and in San Antonio, at a conference Derek was encouraging me. While I was building the company, he was tearing it apart from the inside.

Reporting to the police was procedurally easy, but emotionally terrifying. I sat down at the desk of an APD detective and told him everything—the amount, the date, the name of the fake company, Derek’s last known address, his mother’s name in Fort Worth—and the detective typed it all on a form with two fingers and told me that a wire fraud case of this magnitude would likely go to federal jurisdiction and that I should be prepared for a slow process.

“How slow?” I asked. He looked at me. “It could take a year. If he’s out of state, it could take longer.” I drove back to the office and sat in the parking lot for a long time. The sun was doing a dramatic thing over the mountains, a rainbow of orange and copper. 

The same sky Derek and I had seen standing on the roof of our first office when we were twenty-six, unassuming and determined to build something that would make sense. I went inside and called my civil lawyer to talk about the case.


The civil case was moving faster than the criminal case. My lawyer, a petite woman named Priya Desai, who had the energy to really enjoy this kind of fight, filed the case within a week. Derek had been wrong—the shell company was weak, the transactions were traceable, and he had left behind documents that he was sure I would never see. 

That confidence cost him dearly. Within three weeks, a judge had ordered the seizure of his known assets. There wasn’t much to seize. A truck, which he had registered in his own name. A small investment account, which he had probably forgotten about. But the seizure meant that he couldn’t sell his assets and escape in immediate contempt of court.

Priya looked at the calculations and told me that our case was strong. “He’s not going to get away with this easily,” she said. We sat in her office on South Congress, the coffee between us getting cold. "The question is, how much money can we actually recover, and when?"

"How much?" He tilted his head. The most realistic scenario? Sixty, maybe seventy thousand. And it won't be quick. Sixty thousand dollars versus two hundred dollars. I did the math in the car. I did it again when I got home. 

I was still doing the math until two in the morning, staring at a spreadsheet that had become something between a financial document and a wound.

The Meridian deal was canceled. Sandra Chu sent a professional, thoughtful email explaining that this volatility was too much of a risk for her board to handle. I read the email three times. I couldn't be mad at her. She was right.

What saved the company — somehow — was a freight broker named Lionel Park, who had known me for two years and had heard through industry whispers that I was in trouble. He was the one who called me, not me. 

He had the capital and the connections, and he had zero tolerance for the kind of loose financial structure that I and Derek were operating under. His first condition before any partnership discussions was a full audit. 

His second condition was dual-signature approval for every transaction over five hundred dollars. “I’ve seen it before,” Lionel said the day we sat down to discuss it. “It’s not usually best friends. But the relationship is the same. One builds, the other watches.”

“Who was I?” He looked at me without much sympathy but with a kind of respect I hadn’t felt in months. “You were the one who put relationships above profits. That’s over.”

He wasn’t wrong. We reorganized, rebuilt our client list from scratch, and spent the next eight months making a profit that would have put our old selves to shame. The civil judgment was ultimately for sixty-three thousand dollars. 

It took Priya another year and my patience to collect. Derek had appeared briefly in Nevada, then disappeared again. The last time I checked, the federal wire fraud case was still ongoing. As the detective had said, the process was slow. I never spoke to him again. Not a word.

What I didn’t know at twenty-six, watching the sun set over Austin, is this: Loyalty isn’t a character trait that you either have or don’t have. It’s a structure you build and maintain. Contracts, controls, transparency, verification — these are not insults to the people you trust. They are the architecture that sustains trust.

If you build something with someone you love, someone you’ve known your whole life, someone you’d bet everything on, then create the paperwork. Audit the accounts. Require signatures. The person who never mentions these safeguards is the one who plans to use their absence against you.
